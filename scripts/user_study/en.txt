Have you ever wondered how your phone stores thousands of photographs without running out of space? Every digital photograph is made of tiny coloured squares called pixels. Each pixel contains information about its colour. When millions of pixels come together, they create a photograph, but they also require a lot of storage space. So, how can we make these images smaller without making them look terrible? That's where image compression becomes useful.

Look at these three photographs. They show the same image, but their file sizes are very different. The first occupies 262 kilobytes, while the second takes only 22 kilobytes. That's almost twelve times smaller! The third takes only 6 kilobytes, but notice how much detail has disappeared. This shows the challenge of compression: reducing file size while keeping the image looking good. The smaller file is not always the better image.

JPEG stands for Joint Photographic Experts Group. It is a widely used method for compressing photographs. JPEG commonly uses something called lossy compression. This means some original image information is permanently removed. But here's the clever part: JPEG tries to remove details that our eyes are less likely to notice. For example, a smooth blue sky contains many similar colours, so JPEG can represent that area more efficiently. Now let's explore how JPEG actually works.

JPEG starts by preparing the image. Most digital photographs use red, green, and blue values, called RGB, to represent colours. JPEG commonly converts this information into brightness and colour components, because our eyes are sensitive to changes in brightness. Next, the image is divided into small blocks, usually eight pixels wide and eight pixels high. That's sixty-four pixels in each block. Imagine cutting a photograph into hundreds of tiny squares. JPEG processes these blocks separately.

Now comes an important step called the Discrete Cosine Transform, or DCT. Don't worry about the complicated name. Imagine two small image blocks. One contains a smooth blue sky, while the other contains someone's hair. The sky has gradual changes, while the hair contains many fine details. DCT describes these patterns using frequencies. Low frequencies represent smooth changes, and high frequencies represent rapid changes. This helps JPEG organize the information inside each block.

After DCT, each eight-by-eight block produces sixty-four values called coefficients. One value, called DC, represents the average level of the block. The other sixty-three, called AC coefficients, describe variations and details. Think of DC as the overall brightness and AC as the changes within the square. At this stage, JPEG has reorganized the information, but it hasn't deliberately removed detail yet. That happens next.

The next step is quantization, and it is where JPEG starts losing information. Imagine measuring a pencil as 15.8 centimetres but recording it as 16 centimetres. You have simplified the number while losing a little precision. JPEG does something similar. It divides DCT coefficients by values from a quantization table and rounds the results. Some small values become zero. This reduces detail, especially in patterns that are less important to human vision.

Look at the numbers in this example. One DCT coefficient is approximately negative 711. Dividing it by 16 gives approximately negative 44 after rounding. Notice that many other values in the resulting table have become zeros. These zeros make the image easier to compress. However, stronger quantization removes more detail. That is why a highly compressed JPEG photograph can sometimes look blurry or blocky.

Now JPEG has many simplified numbers, including lots of zeros. To organize them, it uses zig-zag scanning. Instead of reading the table row by row, JPEG follows the zig-zag pattern shown here. This puts lower-frequency information first and generally moves many zeros toward the end. Why is that useful? Because long sequences of zeros are easier to represent efficiently.

Next comes run-length encoding. Imagine writing zero eight times in a row. Instead, you could simply describe the sequence as eight zeros. That's the basic idea. JPEG efficiently records runs of zeros between nonzero coefficients, reducing the amount of information needed. Unlike quantization, this step does not throw away additional image details. It simply stores the remaining information more efficiently.

JPEG also commonly uses Huffman coding. Imagine creating a secret code where frequently used symbols get shorter labels, while rare symbols get longer labels. Huffman coding follows this idea using binary codes made of zeros and ones. Frequently occurring symbols receive shorter codes, which helps reduce file size. When combined with the previous steps, this produces the compressed JPEG data.

Let's return to these three photographs. The first is uncompressed, the second is compressed, and the third is compressed even more. Notice how stronger compression saves space but can reduce visual quality. Now remember the main JPEG steps: prepare the image, divide it into blocks, apply DCT, perform quantization, organize the numbers using zig-zag scanning, and encode them efficiently. Together, these steps make photographs smaller. Here is one final question to think about: why does stronger JPEG compression sometimes make an image blurry or blocky? The answer is quantization, because that is where some original image detail is permanently lost.
